I. Praise, Real History, and Key Symbols

Civilian Defeat in August 1945 Wiesbaden

Most World War II novels focus on front-line battles. This book starts three months after the surrender. Defeated German civilians live in a "provisional purgatory." People eat a spoonful of lard for breakfast and brush their teeth with their fingers. Shoes have the toes cut off to make them fit longer. Women stand in long bread lines with ration stamps at Frau Hermann's bakery, and workers drink lukewarm saltwater bouillon made from cubes sent by the director's wife from America. Readers praise this raw, unvarnished look at civilian hunger, rubble trains (Trümmerbahn), and military occupation.

The Monuments Men and Merkers Mine Treasures

The novel connects directly to the real history of the U.S. Army Monuments, Fine Arts, and Archives (MFAA) section at the Wiesbaden Landesmuseum. Fifty trucks arrive from the Merkers salt mine smelling of mine salt. The crates contain real historical masterworks: Rembrandt's The Money Changer and the 3,300-year-old painted limestone bust of Queen Nefertiti. Captain Cooper secretly opens Nefertiti's crate for Anna, Amalia, and Oskar, showing them that great art endures long after dictators fall.

The Snow Queen and the Devil's Mirror

Captain Cooper gives six-year-old Amalia a copy of Hans Christian Andersen’s The Snow Queen. The fairy tale runs through the whole novel. The devil's shattered mirror represents the moral blindness of the Nazi regime, where people saw only ugliness and lies. At the end, Amalia reminds Anna that roses bloom from underneath the earth where tears fall on graves. This symbol foreshadows how love and courage can grow out of the ruins of war.

II. Character Controversies and Reader Debates

Character / Topic Core Conflict & Reader Arguments Thematic Meaning
Anna Klein's Parenting Anna worries constantly about Amalia's safety. Yet, she leaves Amalia sitting on a street bench outside the museum in 90-degree heat until the child gets sunstroke. She leaves her with Frieda Schilling without checking her past. Later, she hangs an emergency house key on a red ribbon around Amalia's neck and expects her to walk home alone. Readers debate whether Anna is a reckless mother or simply an exhausted refugee facing starvation. Shows the impossible choices facing single mothers in post-war ruins where survival requires taking terrible daily risks.
Captain Henry Cooper Cooper is an architect from Iowa who wants to do the right thing. Some readers love his optimism and his care for Amalia. Others find him reckless and bossy. He ignores military protocol, hides a secret repository of paintings at the Dotzheim villa, loses his Colt .45 sidearm, skips a formal hearing in Frankfurt, and pulls Anna into a dangerous sting at the Nassauer Hof hotel. Examines the clash between strict military bureaucracy (Major Phillips) and personal moral conviction in the field.
The Romance Subplot Readers disagree about the subtle connection between Anna and Cooper. Some enjoyed the slow-burning tension between two lonely people. Others felt the romantic hints were forced and unnecessary, arguing the stolen art mystery and civilian survival plot were strong enough on their own. Questions whether romance belongs in a story about severe war trauma and divided loyalties.
Emil Schilling's Guilt Emil is a decorated Wehrmacht veteran who lost three fingers to frostbite at Leningrad. He cannot escape the memory of an Einsatzgruppen soldier shooting a mother and her baby in a Ukrainian field. He drinks Nordhäuser schnapps to quiet his mind. He steals the Runge painting to expose the truth, attacks his unrepentant sister Frieda with a silver letter opener, and hangs himself in his jail cell. Readers debate whether Emil is a tragic hero or a guilty bystander. Explores the unbearable weight of survivor guilt and the moral destruction of ordinary German soldiers.

III. Editorial Details and Book Trivia

Continuity and Historical Glitches

Sharp readers spotted minor errors. Anna twirls a Lucky Strike cigarette by its "filter," but World War II Lucky Strikes were unfiltered. In early chapters, neighbor Frau Mueller is accidentally called Frau Bucholtz on the next page. Major Phillips is occasionally referred to as a captain.

Wiesbaden Street Names

Some readers felt bogged down by repeated mentions of local German streets—Adolfsallee, Rheinstrasse, Wilhelmstrasse, Schwalbacher Strasse, and Gustav-Freytag-Strasse—without enough map context for readers outside Germany.

The Book Title

The title The Roses Underneath appears only once in Chapter 22, during Amalia's bedtime conversation about The Snow Queen. Readers noted that the title is poetic and subtle, but never fully explained in the text.

IV. Book Club Discussion Questions

  1. The Letter from Thomas: In Chapter 25, Anna receives a letter from her husband Thomas. He is running a hospital in Kappellendorf under the Soviet Kommandant, eating fresh horsemeat, and asking her to return. Why does Anna choose to stay in Wiesbaden instead? Was this decision selfish or brave?
  2. The Power of Names: Konrad Schenk turns out to be Gerhart Heinrich, a notorious art looter who faked his death. Why was it so easy for former Nazis and SS officers to hide in plain sight after the war?
  3. Frieda and the SS Pin: Amalia finds an SS Helferin lightning-bolt pin in a dress-up trunk. How does Frieda justify her wartime past as a Lebensborn nurse? What does her character show about ordinary people who supported the regime?
  4. Oskar's Healing: Oskar begins the novel as an angry boy who wanted to bomb Allied cities. What causes him to change and trust Anna? What role do his mother's landscape paintings play in his healing?
  5. The Meaning of Restitution: Returning stolen art was not simple. Corrupt Americans like Major Phillips and Corporal Miller wanted bribes, while dealers like Ludwig Schneider stripped gallery labels. Does the novel paint an honest picture of the Allied occupation?

Chapters 6–8
(pp. 41–80)		 Captain Cooper drafts Anna as his translator for building surveys. They drive a jeep to an abandoned villa in Dotzheim. In an upstairs wardrobe, they find eight-year-old orphan Oskar Grünewald hiding with a pocket knife, bottle opener, and pencil. In the cellar, they find a secret stash of paintings. Anna identifies a large canvas by German Romantic Philipp Otto Runge (The Hülsenbeck Children) with a "Galerie Breuer Darmstadt" label. Cooper takes photos with his cracked Kodak camera. They take Oskar to the UNRRA DP camp, where refugee Maria Niemeyer promises to look after him. Monuments, Fine Arts, and Archives (MFAA) field work; U.S. Military Government Paragraph 51 property protections; UNRRA displaced persons camps; the September 1944 firebombing of Darmstadt.
Chapters 9–12
(pp. 81–120)		 Art dealer Ludwig Schneider brings a recommendation letter from Major Phillips in Frankfurt asking for a job at the CCP. Cooper refuses because Schneider was in the SA. Anna visits Auntie Madeleine at St. Josef's hospital and brings her an egg and quince jam. Madeleine gets discharged for talking back to the head nurse. At 4:15 AM, Cooper bangs on Anna's door covered in blood. Someone ambushed him in the villa basement, stole his Colt .45 pistol, and took the Runge painting. Cooper investigates Oskar's papers and finds out Oskar was adopted through the SS Lebensborn program in Steinhöring, Bavaria. De-Nazification vetting of gallery dealers; the SS Lebensborn breeding program; black-market art trafficking; clashes between front-line MFAA officers and Frankfurt headquarters brass.
Chapters 13–15
(pp. 121–160)		 Anna sees Schneider carrying a package on Schwalbacher Strasse and tails him to an apartment building with nameplates for Fromm, Schenk, and Knopf. On Saturday, Anna, Cooper, Amalia, and Oskar ride bikes to the Neroberg hill. They visit the Russian chapel and eat apples and Hershey bars. Anna confesses that her husband Thomas is a dedicated communist doctor in Thuringia. Cooper proposes a sting operation: Anna will offer a landscape painting to Schneider to catch him breaking Paragraph 51. Meanwhile, Oskar breaks down and tells Anna how his mother Magda died when their Darmstadt cellar collapsed during the 1944 bombing. Arrival of Berlin museum treasures from the Merkers salt mine; civilian fear of Soviet zone repatriation; enforcement and defiance of military non-fraternization rules.
Chapters 16–18
(pp. 161–200)		 Two MPs arrest Anna at her apartment and take her to USFET headquarters for four hours of harsh questioning about Cooper's missing reports. The next evening, wearing Uncle Otto's old wool pants and wristwatch, Anna takes a Rhine landscape from the villa to the Nassauer Hof hotel. Schneider and his partner "Konrad Schenk" meet her in a bedroom. Schenk recognizes the painting instantly, exposes Anna's trap, and threatens her family. Cooper fails to show up because he saw Schneider's lookouts following Anna to the church. Schenk shoves Anna into the hallway, and she throws up on the hotel carpet. USFET military tribunals; criminal networks operating out of luxury hotels like the Nassauer Hof; forged transit documents used for black-market border crossings.
Chapters 19–20
(pp. 201–220)		 Major Phillips comes to Wiesbaden, screams at Cooper for insubordination, and banishes him to count supplies at the Erbenheim airfield. Schneider takes over an office at the CCP. Frau Obersdorfer sends Anna back to the typing pool. Outside the museum fence, Schenk offers Anna unstamped travel papers from Weimar to Wiesbaden for $300, threatening to report Thomas as a communist if she refuses. Amalia invites Emil Schilling to dinner at Adolfsallee. Emil brings dark bread, cheese, and Nordhäuser schnapps, promising Anna he will protect her and Amalia from his sister. Military corruption and favoritism; forged travel permits; deep trauma and alcoholism among Wehrmacht veterans who survived Operation Barbarossa and Stalingrad.
Chapters 21–22
(pp. 221–240)		 Anna sneaks into the file room and intercepts a phone call from Major Phillips' secretary, who needs an appraiser for a large painting of a girl on a chair with a torn Darmstadt label (the Runge). Anna steals an Army jeep from the museum courtyard, drives to the DP camp, and finds out Oskar ran away. At the Dotzheim villa, Anna finds $300 in cash hidden in the wardrobe, but corrupt Corporal Miller catches her and tears her blouse. Anna flees to Cooper and Corporal Bender at the airfield. Meanwhile, Amalia finds an SS Helferin lightning-bolt pin in a toy trunk. Frieda hits Amalia, Emil pulls Cooper's stolen gun on Frieda, and Anna arrives just in time to rescue Amalia. Military supply graft; complicity of female SS auxiliaries (SS-Helferinnen); underground childcare dangers in bombed cities.
Chapters 23–25
(pp. 241–297)		 A formal military hearing dismisses Anna when Phillips and Schenk collude against her. At the Albrechtstrasse jail, a guard tells Anna that Emil hanged himself in his cell using his torn shirt. Emil leaves Anna his Rhine landscape painting. Anna pulls off tape on the canvas stretcher and finds Cooper's developed camera photo of the Runge painting with "Good luck, Anna" written on the back. Oskar reveals that Schenk is actually his uncle, master Nazi art looter Gerhart Heinrich of Galerie Neustadt, and that Oskar's mother Magda painted the landscapes. Anna brings the proof to Director Farmer. The MPs arrest Schneider in the workshop, Heinrich is exposed, and Miller's bribes are halted. Cooper sneaks Anna, Amalia, and Oskar in to see the bust of Queen Nefertiti. Thomas writes asking Anna to join him in the Soviet zone, but Anna refuses. Promoted to a Level-Two government worker with housing assistance, Anna chooses to stay in Wiesbaden with Amalia and adopt Oskar. Full exposure of the Heinrich and Schneider art theft ring; genuine family restitution; moral independence under Western Allied occupation; rejecting Soviet totalitarianism to protect a child's future.

Character Profiles

﻿

Anna Klein

Protagonist / Translator

A resilient Austrian refugee living in one room at Adolfsallee 45. She starts her mornings eating a spoonful of lard and brushing her teeth with her finger while nursing a wobbly tooth. Formerly an apprentice at her uncle's art gallery in Salzburg, she speaks fluent English. Wearing dead Uncle Otto's oversized pinstripe wool pants and wristwatch, she steals an Army jeep, faces down Nazi art thieves, and rejects returning to her communist husband in the Soviet zone to build a free life with Amalia and Oskar.

Captain Henry "Coop" Cooper

MFAA Arts Officer

An architect from Chicago who grew up on an Iowa farm. He volunteered for the Monuments Men to save historic buildings and art across Europe. Armed with a cracked Kodak 35 camera, a pack of chewing gum, and an upbeat American attitude, he hates military red tape. Even after Major Phillips exiles him to count supplies at Erbenheim airfield, Cooper stands by Anna and secretly opens the wooden crate of Queen Nefertiti for the children.

Amalia Klein

Anna's 6-Year-Old Daughter

A clever girl who wears mismatched socks inside dusty shoes with the toes cut off. She carries Lulu, a dirty doll missing an eye and hair, and wears a heart-shaped metal dirndl button on a green silk ribbon. She proudly wears her apartment door key on a red ribbon like a medal. Her love for Hans Christian Andersen's The Snow Queen and her discovery of Frieda's hidden SS pin crack the whole mystery wide open.

Oskar Grünewald

Key Witness / Orphan

An eight-year-old orphan found hiding in an upstairs wardrobe at the Dotzheim villa with a pocket knife, a bottle opener, and a pencil. Adopted through the SS Lebensborn home in Steinhöring, he survived the fiery September 1944 bombing of Darmstadt beneath his dying mother Magda. Forced by his uncle to guard stolen paintings, he is the boy shepherd in his mother's Rhine landscape and finds a real home with Anna.

Gerhart Heinrich ("Konrad Schenk")

Primary Antagonist

The former owner of Galerie Neustadt in Mainz and a top art looter for the Nazi ERR. He faked his death certificate and stole the identity of dead restorer Konrad Schenk. He abandoned his nephew Oskar at the villa to guard his loot, bribed Major Phillips with Runge's stolen masterwork, and tried to extort $300 from Anna before being exposed by his dead sister's signature.

Ludwig Schneider

Collaborator / Art Dealer

A sweating, nervous former SA member and art dealer from Frankfurt. He helped steal Jewish art collections, tore off original gallery labels, and claimed the Dotzheim villa repository was his late wife's private property. He tried to bribe Anna with a basket of food and American cigarettes. In the end, MPs handcuff him and drag him out of the museum workshop.

Emil Schilling

Tragic Wehrmacht Veteran

A 22-year-old soldier who lost three fingers to frostbite at Leningrad. Haunted by nightmares of an SS executioner shooting a mother and baby in a Ukrainian field, he drinks Nordhäuser schnapps to quiet his mind. He stole the Runge painting and Cooper's Colt .45 to stop Heinrich. After stabbing his sister Frieda, he hangs himself in the Albrechtstrasse jail, leaving Anna the camera photo hidden behind his canvas.

Frieda Schilling

Antagonist / Lebensborn Nurse

Emil's tall sister who watches neighborhood children at her Gustav-Freytag-Strasse home, serving apple strudel and Nescafé. Beneath her pleasant act, she remains a fanatic Nazi who served as an SS Helferin and Lebensborn breeder at the Dotzheim villa. When Amalia finds her double-lightning-bolt pin, Frieda strikes the girl, provoking the violent fight that leaves her stabbed in the throat.

Thomas Klein

Anna's Husband

An idealistic Austrian doctor who survived water torture and knocked-out teeth from the Gestapo. Instead of fleeing west with his family, he stayed behind in Kappellendorf to serve the Soviet Kommandant. His final letter, boasting of running a Soviet hospital and eating fresh horsemeat, proves to Anna that politics matter more to him than his family, sealing their separation forever.
